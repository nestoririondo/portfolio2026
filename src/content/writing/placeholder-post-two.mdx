In a [recent episode](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LfGenpmOjDQ) of *The Daily*, Steven Strogatz, a mathematician at Cornell, describes the vibe in the academic math community: it's "discombobulated." An OpenAI model has cracked Navier–Stokes, one of the Millennium Prize Problems, and released hundreds of papers on questions that had resisted mathematicians for decades. Understandably, mathematicians don't know how to feel about it.

Neither does Strogatz. At some point in the conversation, he moves from Navier–Stokes to Ecclesiastes. Everything is beautiful in its time, he says. Life is short and unfair. He's a bit of a mess, and he doesn't always make sense. After a longer silence than podcasts usually allow, the journalist is in tears.

"Am I getting to you?" Strogatz asks.

It is one of the most beautiful and touching moments I have heard on a podcast.

How does a conversation about mathematics end up here? For most of the half hour, Strogatz is simply a man in love with his work. He talks about the crickets of Ithaca, thousands of them chirping in unison, and about how the same mathematics explains the cells that keep a heart beating in sync. He talks about the pleasure of being stumped and the moment when everything clicks into place: a feeling mathematicians chase like an addiction. He describes an old photograph of Paul Erdős, the gray-haired wanderer who couldn't make toast, sitting beside an eight-year-old Terence Tao. Both are bent over pencil and paper, working on a problem for the pleasure of being together.

Then, little by little, grief enters the conversation. In May, an AI solved one of Erdős's old puzzles, elegantly, in the way mathematicians dream of solving things. In September came Navier–Stokes. Mathematicians like to think of themselves as heroes, Strogatz admits, and it is hard to be a hero if you can no longer be the first. What AI is taking away is not just their problems. It is the click, and the company of the people with whom you chase it.

At a town-hall meeting in his department, a student at the back of the room asked what everyone was thinking: if AI can do math better than any human, what's the point? She could learn from it, sure. But she wanted to discover things, too. She didn't want her life to be in vain.

Strogatz didn't answer her that day. Instead, he went looking somewhere unexpected: an old song by the Byrds, "Turn! Turn! Turn!", and the book its lyrics come from, Ecclesiastes.

I knew that song. Years ago, during my master's in contemporary philosophy, the philosopher José Luis Pardo played it in a seminar called "Time and Meaning" to explain two ancient concepts of time: Chronos and Kairos.

Chronos is the time of the train timetable. The train leaves at 9:14, whether you are on your way to a wedding or a funeral. Chronological time is an empty container: things happen within it, but it means nothing by itself. One hour is like any other.

Kairos is the time when something is ready. A fig ripens when it ripens. A joke lands at one moment and dies a second later. A book sits unread on your nightstand for years—until one day, you can't put it down.

Kairos can't be scheduled or rushed. It is the time of opportunity, a kind of time we have almost forgotten how to talk about: a time to reap, a time to love, a time to grow. Time and meaning together. This is the time the Byrds were singing about.

Everything is beautiful in its time. We get a turn: to be in love, to be creative, to do what is meaningful, to walk the dog. Before it, we are dust; after it, dust again. The beautiful part, Strogatz says, is the in-between.

Those weeks spent staring at a problem without a clue are part of the in-between, too. They are the time before the click, and they matter because the click might come.

And that is the problem. AI seems to live entirely in Chronos. Its achievements are measured in compute time and tokens spent; it does not need to wait for anything to ripen. It can hand you the answer before you have even learned to wonder about the question. If you never get the thrill, the learning, and the growth that come from solving something yourself, what is left of the in-between?

Strogatz wonders whether mathematicians are the canary in the coal mine for all creative work: poetry, journalism, medicine. I think the canary has already sung, and it was a software developer. In the YouTube comments on the episode, a programmer writes that he loved being stumped, figuring things out for himself, and building something beautiful—and that all of it is gone now. I write software for a living. I know what he means.

So what is the point? I don't know. Strogatz doesn't know either. All he knows is where he wants to start: by reading an old book with a student who is afraid her life will be in vain.

I will have to think about it. It may take some time. The other kind of time.
